field notes on leading engineers

How I think.

Principles aren't trophies — they're scars. Each of these was earned the hard way across fifteen years of building software and the organisations behind it.

01

Design the organisation as carefully as the software.

Team boundaries, ownership, and decision-making have architectural consequences. A well-designed system still struggles when every change requires coordination across too many teams.

So I work on both at once: autonomous teams, explicit responsibilities, fewer dependencies. Organisational design is part of the engineering work — not an HR side-quest.

02

Connect technical work to the business.

Engineering is an engine of growth. The important question is what a technical decision enables: a reliable transaction, a faster customer journey, or a new product the business can sustain.

I make the trade-offs visible and ground the roadmap in outcomes. If a refactor can't tell me what it buys the business, it waits.

03

Modernise with a clear reason.

Legacy systems carry business history as well as technical debt. Modernisation needs a purpose: removing a bottleneck, restoring reliability, or making future change easier.

From critical payment flows to a complete infrastructure separation, my aim is always the same: a simpler, more resilient system that supports the next stage of the business.

04

Make accountability a shared practice.

Predictable delivery depends on clear goals, honest feedback, and an environment where people can grow. Hiring standards, mentorship, and performance expectations belong in the same conversation.

Clear ownership plus structured feedback lets teams decide with confidence — and actually understand the impact of their work.

05

Keep useful knowledge in circulation.

Translating Addy Osmani's Leading Effective Engineering Teams into Persian is another way to contribute to engineering practice. Lessons become more useful when more people can work with them.
